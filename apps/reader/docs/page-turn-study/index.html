In short

  • Grip. Most turns hold the fore-edge near its middle. That bends the page around a line roughly parallel to the spine, close to a cylinder across the whole height. A corner grip makes a cone. Because paper can't stretch, the line where the page leaves the stack is always straight (C5).
  • Shape. Paper can hold itself out for only about 5–10 cm against its own weight, and a book page is roughly twice that wide. So a lifted page arcs softly. The prototype's roll radius, 0.075–0.15 of the page width, is three to five times too tight (C8, C9).
  • Motion. A page let go early falls back under gravity and its gutter's spring, slowed by air, and lands without a bounce. Today a 0.52 s spring drives the finger's offset instead. A committed turn ignores touches until the next page is ready (C15–C19, C38).
  • Material. Book paper is matte and lit by the whole room. Its shading is broad, its shadows soft and warm, and nothing on it shines. The prototype adds a Phong highlight to the back of the leaf, leaves the back smooth, and casts black shadows. That is what reads as magazine (§2.7).
  • Ink. Printed ink sits in the paper. It takes the paper's tooth, spreads about a tenth of a millimetre, and prints a softer black. Only letterpress adds a bite. Today the text is the browser's flat glyphs over a grain layer (C31–C35).
  • Rendering. HTML-in-Canvas is the clean long-term route, but it isn't in Safari or iOS. WebKit started building it in June 2026, behind a flag. I recommend fixing the motion and material on today's hybrid first (stages 1–3). If the flat page still looks printed-on after that, draw the page ourselves in WebGL from the Reader's layout, with invisible DOM text on top (stage 4). A spike at the end shows the drawn ink lands on exactly the same pixels as the DOM text in Chrome and iOS Safari, so that route is open; its speed on a real phone is still to measure.
Part 1

Anatomy of a real page turn

I can't watch video, so this part comes from research on page turning and paper, from mechanics, and from careful description. Section 1.7 gives four frames and a filming recipe so you can check it against your own hands.

1.1 Where the hand goes

C1
JudgementCheck

Most turns start at the fore-edge, between about a third and two thirds of the way down. The thumb or the side of a finger lifts the edge or pushes it toward the spine. Corner turns happen too, usually from the bottom corner, when a book lies on a table or the reader is skimming.

I found no study that counts grip positions. Marshall & Bly (2005) videotaped people reading paper and digital books, but they report navigation, not grips. Liesaputra & Witten (2012) built and tested a page-turning e-book without measuring hands. Check: film twenty of your own turns and note where the thumb is.

C2
Source

Hong, Card & Chen's page-turning model from PARC turns a page from its corner by wrapping it around a cone. The cone's angle starts at 90° (flat), narrows to a minimum by mid-turn and opens again, while the apex slides along the binding. It models the corner turn. It doesn't model a middle grip.

I read their patent (US 7,898,541), which describes the method in detail. I couldn't read the 2006 paper itself.

C3
Judgement

A thumb holding a phone rests naturally in the lower third of the screen. Today the grab height maps straight onto how far the bottom corner leads (lead: 0.26 of the width, tilt up to 35°, in curl-geometry.ts), and a tap holds the page at 72% of its height. So most phone turns come out as corner turns. That fits the feedback that the curl is "an exaggerated way of pulling from the corner".

1.2 Paper bends, but doesn't stretch

C4
SourcePhysics

At the forces of a page turn paper is practically inextensible. So a page can only take developable shapes: surfaces made of straight lines (rulings) along which the paper stays flat. Cylinders, cones and smooth blends of the two are all developable. Bo & Wang (2007) model bending paper exactly this way.

C5
Physics

The line where a lifted page leaves the stack is always straight. A developable surface has the same tangent plane all along each ruling, and a different one on the next ruling. So the part of the page still tangent to the stack ends on a single ruling, which is a straight line. The fore-edge can curve in the air, but the lift line can't.

C6
PhysicsJudgement

Middle grip: the rulings run roughly parallel to the spine, so the page rolls across its full height like a cylinder. The lift line tilts when the hand moves up or down. I'd cap the tilt near 10° unless the grip is at a corner. Corner grip: the rulings fan out from an apex beyond the held corner. The corner curls tightest and the far end of the page barely lifts.

1.3 What shapes the free part: stiffness, gravity, spine

C7
Source

Book paper is bound with its grain, the direction most fibres lie, running parallel to the spine. Bookbinders do this so the book opens flat and the pages turn easily. A page bent across the grain, around a line parallel to the spine, bends more easily than one bent the other way. Wrong-grain books make pages that resist turning and a spine that cracks.

C8
Physics

Paper can hold itself out only so far against its own weight. That distance is the elastogravity length, ℓ = (B/ρg)1/3. For book paper it's about 5–10 cm. A trade paperback page is 11–14 cm wide, about twice that. So a page neither stands stiff like card nor flops like cloth. It arcs.

B = E·t³/12, with E ≈ 2–4 GPa (bulky book paper, bent across the grain) and caliper t ≈ 90–120 µm, so B ≈ 1.2–5.8 × 10⁻⁴ N·m. Grammage 60–80 g/m² gives ρg ≈ 0.59–0.78 N/m². Then ℓ ≈ 5.4–9.9 cm. A typical 70 g/m² sheet comes out at 6–8 cm. Munken Print Cream at 80 g/m² is 120 µm thick, a bulk of 1.5 cm³/g, so book papers are thick for their weight. The same length scale sets the fold height of thin sheets in a recent paper on elasto-gravity folding.
C9
PhysicsJudgement

A lifted page spreads its bend over most of its width. The radius of curvature is comparable to ℓ, several centimetres, roughly 0.3–0.6 of the page width. The prototype's roll starts at 0.075 and grows to 0.15 of the width (SOFT_CURL, curl-geometry.ts:33–38). On a 390 pt screen that's a 29–58 pt tube. A loop that tight only forms when a finger slides the page flat across the stack instead of lifting it.

C10
SourceJudgement

The leaf is hinged at the gutter. A sewn leaf hinges freely and lies flat on either side. A glued paperback leaf has a spring at the gutter that tries to close the book, more so when the book is new. I'd model the gutter as a weak torsion spring whose rest angle is upright (the closed book), so a turned page leans back a little. Its strength would be adjustable, from sewn to glued.

1.4 Sag at the corners

C11
PhysicsCheck

When a page is held at one point of its fore-edge, the top and bottom corners have nothing under them, so they droop and trail behind. The paper stays developable by forming two shallow cones that fan out from the grip, as a sheet does when you hold it up by one point. From above, the fore-edge bows: it leads at the grip and lags at the corners, by a few millimetres on a real page. The grain helps here, because the stiffer direction runs up the page. So the sag is small, but it's what tells a held page from a rigid card.

Check: lift a page by the middle of its edge and sight along the fore-edge. The demo above doesn't draw the sag; stage 2 would add it.

1.5 Two ways the leaf moves, and what a phone can show

C12
JudgementCheck

One turn mixes two motions. Lift: the leaf rotates about the spine in a gentle arc. From above, the front of the page tips toward you and its lines crowd toward the spine. The next page appears beneath it in soft shadow. The back of the leaf hardly shows until the leaf passes upright. Peel: the thumb slides the edge toward the spine while the part near the spine stays on the stack. The page loops and shows its back on top of the loop, the classic "page curl". Most turns, I think, start with a little peel and finish as a lift. How much of each your own turns use is the first thing to check against film.

C13
PhysicsJudgement

A phone shows one page, and its spine is the left edge of the screen. So the phone can only show the half of each turn that happens over the right-hand page. A forward turn leaves the screen as the leaf stands up at the spine. A backward turn enters with the leaf already upright at the spine.

C14
Judgement

On a phone, the page revealed under the turning leaf is the next page. So each leaf is effectively printed on one side only. Its back is blank paper with its own front's text showing faintly through, mirrored. It's a convenient fiction, like a single-sided proof, and the prototype already makes this choice.

1.6 Letting go, catching, turning back

C15
Physics

Let go before upright, a page falls back. Gravity and the gutter's spring drive it, and air drag limits its speed. A 65 g/m² sheet falling flat tops out near 1 m/s. So after a short acceleration the fore-edge travels at a fairly steady speed, and a fall from 45° takes a few tenths of a second.

Terminal speed of a flat sheet: v = √(2ρg / (ρ_air·C_d)) = √(2 × 0.065 × 9.81 / (1.2 × 1.2)) ≈ 0.94 m/s, and 0.7 m/s if C_d ≈ 2. A rigid 12 cm leaf hinged at the spine with no drag would fall from 45° in about 0.13 s. With drag and bending it takes longer. The simulation below lands from 45° in the time it prints.
C16
Physics

It lands softly. The air under the leaf has to squeeze out sideways, which brakes the last few millimetres, and paper's internal friction absorbs what's left. There's no bounce and no overshoot. A zero-bounce spring gets the "no overshoot" part right but the timing wrong: a spring slows down all the way in, while a falling page speeds up and then brakes on the cushion of air at the end.

C17
Physics

Let go past upright, gravity carries it over the spine. Drag acts most on the fast-moving outer part, so the fore-edge trails and the leaf bows against the motion.

C18
Physics

A flick before upright turns the page if it carries the leaf past upright: if its kinetic energy is more than the work needed to lift the leaf's centre to the top. This is the physical form of "position and velocity decide the outcome" (Plate 13).

C19
JudgementCheck

A falling page can be caught wherever a finger meets it. It stops at that point and hangs there, and the rest of the leaf settles around the finger. It doesn't jump so that its edge is under the finger.

C20
Judgement

To turn back in a book, you lift the last turned leaf from the left-hand stack. On the phone that leaf enters at the spine already upright (C13). The finger then carries its fore-edge to the right and lays it down, with the same physics in reverse.

1.7 Four frames to check against film

How to check this with your own book

Use iPhone Slo-mo at 240 fps. Lay a matte paperback open on a table, lit by daylight from a window plus normal room light. Film from two places. First, straight down onto the right-hand page, like the phone's own view. Second, from the foot of the book at page height, like Plate 6.

Make 10 middle turns, 5 bottom-corner turns, 5 turns let go at about 45°, and 5 turns caught in mid-air. Then pause on frames that match 1–4 above and note:

  • the bend's radius at mid-turn as a fraction of the page width (C9);
  • the lift line's tilt, and whether it stays straight (C5, C6);
  • how much of the page's back you see from above (C12);
  • the time from letting go to rest, and any overshoot (C15, C16);
  • the width of the darkened band beside the curl compared with how high the paper is (C24).

If you share one clip, I can turn these into numbers for stage 1.

Book paper as a material

Uncoated book paper (wove, cream or white, about 60–80 g/m²) against the coated paper of a magazine. Then ink on that paper, and why today's curl reads as glossy.

C21
Physics

Matte here means nearly Lambertian. Rough fibres scatter light in every direction, so the paper looks equally bright from any viewing angle. All surfaces pick up some sheen at grazing angles, paper included, but at reading angles it's negligible. So the material needs no specular term at all.

2.2 Light on a curl

C22
Physics

Room light arrives from a large area: windows, ceiling, walls. Under even light from a whole sky, a surface tilted by θ receives (1 + cos θ)/2 of what a flat page receives. So a part of the page standing vertical gets half the light, and the shading changes smoothly across the bend.

Today's lighting curve isn't far from that by itself. It's one lamp plus an ambient of 0.36, which gives 37% at vertical (curl-renderer.ts:76–85). The real differences are the highlight on top of it, which lifts the back of the roll above the brightness of flat paper, and the tight roll, which squeezes the whole curve into a narrow band.

C23
Physics

The inside of a curl is darker because it faces down, away from the room. Its only light is what bounces off the page beneath it, and that page is in the leaf's shadow. Expect about 30–60% of the flat page's brightness there.

C24
Physics

A shadow's soft edge grows with height: its width is about the height of the paper above the page times the angular size of the light. Beside the lift line the leaf touches the page, so the shadow there is narrow and faint. Under the high part it widens into a broad dimming. It never has a hard edge.

C25
Physics

The shadow under a single leaf is never black or grey. The leaf lets about a fifth of the light through (C27), warmed by the paper. So the shadow is a darker, warmer shade of the paper itself. Today's shadow is pure black at up to 38% opacity, and up to 56% in dark themes (curl-renderer.ts:139–144, 413).

2.3 Translucency and show-through

C26
Source

Printers call it show-through when print on the back of a sheet is visible from the front in ordinary reflected light. Printing opacity measures it: the reflectance of a sheet over a solid black print is compared with the same sheet over a pad of blank sheets. Book papers run about 88–94%.

C27
Physics

Show-through doesn't need a light behind the page. Light that enters the front scatters through the sheet, some of it reaches the ink on the back and is absorbed, and so less comes back out. Held up to a light, the same print shows at nearly full contrast.

Kubelka–Munk with reflectance of a thick pad R∞ ≈ 0.85 and opacity 0.89–0.92 gives a single-sheet reflectance R₀ ≈ 0.76–0.78 and transmittance T ≈ 0.17–0.20. In reflected light, a solid black on the back dims the front by about 1 − opacity, so 8–11%. Thin text strokes average lower, about 2–4% over a line of text. With light coming through, ink blocks nearly all of the 17–20% that passes, so the text shows at almost full contrast.
C28
PhysicsJudgement

The prototype mixes 12% of the blurred front into the back in light themes (SHOW_THROUGH, use-page-curl.ts:54). That's at the strong end of the reflected range, but plausible. What's missing is the transmitted light. A raised leaf has light reaching its underside from the room and from the page below. That light comes through, warm, and shows the formation and the mirrored text.

2.4 Formation, fibres and tooth

C29
SourceJudgement

Formation is how evenly the fibres are spread. Hold a sheet up to the light (papermakers call it the look-through) and you see it as cloudiness. In reflected light it's almost invisible on good book paper. So it belongs mostly in the transmitted light, on a raised leaf. Tooth is the fine roughness of the surface. It shows mainly when light grazes the page.

One naming point: the Reader's richer grain is labelled "Laid". Laid paper has regular parallel lines from the mould, called laid and chain lines. The texture there, cloud plus a few fibres, is closer to an antique wove.

2.6 Ink on book paper

C31
Source

Most books today are printed offset or digitally, so the ink is flat: a film a micron or two thick. On uncoated paper the ink's oils soak into the fibres while the pigment stays near the surface. So the print takes on the paper's texture, and dark areas print softer and warmer than on coated paper. The uncoated reference profile allows for 22% mid-tone dot gain.

C32
PhysicsJudgement

Light wanders sideways inside paper before it comes back out, on the order of a tenth of a millimetre. So the edges of a glyph look slightly darker and softer than the ink itself. This is optical dot gain, the Yule–Nielsen effect: light can enter through bare paper and leave through ink, or the other way round. A phone shows a book page at about 0.6 of its printed size, and at 460 ppi 0.1 mm comes to about one device pixel. That reads as softness, not blur.

Model used in the loupe below: R(x) = paper(x) · T(x) · [PSF ∗ T](x), where T is the ink's single-pass transmittance and the PSF is the paper's lateral spread (σ ≈ 0.07 mm).
C33
Source

Letterpress presses raised, inked type into the paper. Traditional printers aimed for a kiss impression: enough pressure for even ink, with no bite you could see or feel. The deep bite is a modern taste, and it rounds serifs and breaks hairlines. Under pressure the ink squeezes toward the edges of the letter, so letter edges print a little darker.

C34
Judgement

The "embossed" feel you're after is letterpress, lit by the room. A shallow bite shows as a thin shadow on the inner edge nearest the light and a thin highlight on the opposite edge. I'd go from a kiss up to a light bite, and keep it a setting. A deep bite reads as a wedding invitation, not a novel.

C35
Judgement

The cheapest step toward ink that sits in the paper needs no WebGL. Today the grain layer is painted under the text: SpreadView renders .reader-paper-grain before the page content, and the glyphs cover it at full strength. Moving the grain above the text with mix-blend-mode: multiply makes the ink take the paper's tooth. A slightly warm, slightly lighter ink does the rest.

Part 3

Interaction model

The page as a physical object you can hold, let go of and catch, instead of an animation of a progress value.

C36
Judgement

The leaf has a state. The state is the shape of a strip across the page (about 24 segments, as in Plate 6), the tilt of its rulings, its velocity, and whether a finger holds it and at which point. The finger is a constraint on the held point, and letting go removes the constraint. What happens next is whatever the physics does. Today the fold is solved from the swipe offset (computeCurlFold), so even after release the shape is drawn as if a finger were still holding the edge, and a spring moves that imaginary finger.

C37
Judgement

Grip band, and grip height chooses the bend. A touch in the right third of the page holds the leaf at the touched point. Between 15% and 85% of the height, the grip is a middle grip: a cylinder whose lift line tilts with the finger's vertical travel, capped at 10°. In the top or bottom 15% it's a corner grip, with a cone whose apex lies beyond that corner. A drag that starts elsewhere on the page still turns it, as today, holding the fore-edge at the touch's height. The finger sets the held point's position across the page, and the model chooses its height. A single lift parameter, from peel to lift (C12), is what we'd tune against film.

C38
Source: code

Where interruption breaks today. In use-spread-swipe-navigation.ts, handlePointerDown returns early while pendingNavigationRef.current is set or the phase is awaiting-navigation. A committed settle dispatches the navigation as soon as it starts, because offset ≤ 0 is already true for a forward turn. So from the moment a turn commits until the worker promotes the next page, a touch does nothing. After promotion the offset is rebased and the turn can be caught without a jump, which is good. I couldn't measure the length of that gap on the phone. Elsewhere, a touch on a settling page has to move 10 px sideways before it holds, and lifting the finger without moving resumes the old target.

C39
Judgement

Catch, reverse, release, at any time. A touch on the moving leaf attaches at the touched point, with the hold eased in over about 50 ms so nothing pops. From there it's an ordinary drag: carry on, reverse or let go. A touch that misses the leaf while it's still on screen attaches to the nearest point of its free edge. Once the leaf has left the screen, a rightward drag brings it back as a backward turn. This needs the page change decoupled from the animation. Either keep both pages' rasters and change the page only when the leaf leaves the screen, or make the change undoable while the turn is still settling.

C40
PhysicsJudgement

Position and velocity decide, through the physics. On a table, the hump a page must cross is at upright. On a phone, upright is the left edge of the screen, too far for a thumb. I'd move the hump to about 40% of the carry, using the model's gutter spring and gravity direction. A slow half-way push then turns the page, and a short push turns it only with a flick. The decision and the motion then agree: the page never "decides" to turn while looking as if it should fall back. Today's rule, a 25% threshold on a 0.1 s velocity projection, is a good ergonomic target to match.

Judgement

Taps. A tap on the right third plays the same physics with a scripted hand: a short push at the tap's height, counted as a middle grip anywhere in the middle 70%. It looks like a flick and can be caught. A tap on the left third brings the previous leaf back the same way. Today a tap holds the page at 72% of its height, which makes every tapped turn lean on the bottom corner.

C42
Judgement

What stays the same. With Reduce Motion on, the page changes at once, as today. Text selection, VoiceOver page turning and the slide style don't change.

Part 4

Rendering options, honestly assessed

The text has to feel printed into the paper, stay crisp and selectable, and run smoothly on an iPhone without draining it.

4.1 HTML-in-Canvas

C43
Source

What it is. A WICG proposal led by Google and Igalia. A canvas opts its children in for drawing. The attribute was layoutsubtree; since Chrome 155 it's content="drawable", plus drawable on each child. The browser lays out and records those children, and the page draws them into the canvas: drawElementImage() in 2D, texElementSubImage2D() in WebGL, drawElementImageToTexture() in WebGPU. A paint event fires when they change. updateElementGeometry() tells the browser where a child ended up, so hit-testing, selection and accessibility follow it. For privacy the snapshot leaves out cross-origin content, visited-link colours, spelling marks, system colours and subpixel antialiasing.

C44
Source

Status on 25 October 2026.

  • Chrome: developer trial behind chrome://flags/#canvas-draw-element since M138. Origin trial from M148, since extended through M160. The API was reshaped in M150 and M155. Not in stable.
  • Safari / WebKit: standards-position issue #630 is open. On 3 August 2026 a WebKit engineer wrote that they'd mark it "support" in a week, pending objections. I couldn't confirm the label was applied. Implementation tracking bug 316240 was opened on 3 June 2026. A feature flag, the IDL, the drawable attribute and WebGL texElementSubImage2D have landed in WebKit's main branch, with a fix as recent as 12 October. Hit-testing, accessibility, the paint event and cross-origin tainting are still open. It isn't in any shipping Safari or in iOS, and no release has been announced.
  • Firefox: no position, no implementation.
  • iPhone: every iOS browser uses WebKit, so nothing on the phone can run it today.
C45
Judgement

What it would give us. The real DOM page as a texture: our fonts, highlights, search marks, pseudo-elements, selection and caret, re-recorded whenever they change. Hit-testing would stay in register for free. The shader could treat that texture as ink coverage and build the paper and impression around it on the GPU, which replaces page-raster.ts and its gaps. Limits: in WebGL we pass the transform ourselves, and a curled page isn't one matrix, so hit-testing wouldn't follow the bend mid-turn. We don't need it to. Verdict: the right long-term tool, but not usable on your phone now. Design stage 4 so it can swap in later.

4.2 Draw the ink into the paper ourselves

C46
Judgement

How. Pretext already decides every line break, fragment, font, indent and word gap in the worker, measuring with the same canvas text engine the page uses. From that layout, or from the DOM's own word boxes as page-raster.ts reads them, draw ink coverage with fillText into a single-channel texture at device resolution. Images go into a second texture. A fragment shader then builds the page from the paper's colour, tooth, formation and fibres, on both sides of the leaf. It multiplies the ink in with about one pixel of spread and the paper's tooth in its density. It lights everything with room light and a soft key, and takes impression normals from the blurred coverage. Above the canvas, the DOM page stays where it is with color: transparent text, for selection, links, VoiceOver and highlights. Highlights use mix-blend-mode: multiply, so they read as ink too.

C47
PhysicsJudgement

Can the invisible text stay in register? I expected so, within a fraction of a device pixel, at rest. Canvas fillText and DOM text shape with the same engine, font and size; in WebKit that's Core Text. The prototype already places runs at the DOM's own Range rectangles. What could still drift:

  • baseline rounding from font metrics;
  • glyph origins snapping differently in DOM and canvas;
  • justification, handled by drawing word by word;
  • fonts that load late;
  • pinch zoom, which needs the canvas re-rendered at the zoomed scale, or the ink blurs while the DOM's selection stays sharp.

Mid-turn, register doesn't matter, because the gesture owns the touch. The spike measured it at rest: with positions read back from the DOM, the drawn ink lands on the same pixels as the DOM text in Chrome and in iOS Safari (Spike).

C48
Judgement

Cost. One fillText pass per page, about 250–400 runs, done while the reader rests, as rasters are today. One texture upload, about 3 MB for a single channel at 1179 × 2556. In the simulator a full-colour upload took 59 ms, once per page; it needs measuring on the phone. Drawing happens once at rest and then only during a turn, so resting costs no battery. A turn is a full-screen shader with about ten texture reads, well within an iPhone GPU at 60–120 Hz. Risks: everything the DOM shows has to be redrawn: images, highlights, search marks, note badges. iOS can drop the WebGL context in the background; the fallback is to make the DOM text visible again. And a second renderer has to stay faithful to the first.

4.3 Today's hybrid, improved

C49
Judgement

Keep the live DOM page, clipped at the crease, and let WebGL draw only the bent part from a snapshot. Fix the material as in Plate 10, and move the grain above the text with multiply (C35). It's cheap, crisp at rest and natively selectable. Its ceiling is lower. Two renderers meet at the crease and must match. The flat page can't be lit, so an impression can't respond to the light or the bend. And the snapshot still misses pseudo-elements, gradients, text shadows and search marks.

Recommendation

Stages 1–3 don't depend on the renderer: the physical leaf, the gesture, the material. Do them on today's hybrid (C), so you can judge motion and paper quickly. Then decide at the gate after stage 3. If the flat page with multiplied ink already feels like paper, stop there. If it still reads as text printed on top, build B for the ink. The spike shows its main risk, register, is solved in Chrome and iOS Safari when the ink is placed from the DOM's own word boxes. Build B's coverage step behind one interface, so HTML-in-Canvas can replace it once WebKit ships it.

When to pause instead: if, after stage 2, your side-by-side with your own film still reads "not paper"; or if B, on your phone, can't keep register within half a device pixel (the simulator keeps it exactly) or costs more than 8 ms a frame. Resume when WebKit ships HTML-in-Canvas.

Spike

Ink in register

A small feasibility test of option B's main risk: ink drawn into a WebGL paper from the Reader's layout, under invisible DOM text, at phone size. Does it land where the DOM's text would?

I built it, ran it in Chrome and in Safari on iOS 27, and measured it. It's a scratch page in the worktree, apps/reader/spikes/ink-register/ on agent/paper-study, with its own dev server. It doesn't touch the Reader.

Measured

S2
Measured

Register isn't the risk. Read each word's position back from the invisible DOM, with a Range box per word and a zero-size inline-block as a baseline probe, and fillText puts the ink on the same pixels as the DOM's glyphs, in both engines. In Safari the two are identical: canvas and page text go through the same Core Text rasteriser. This answers the half-pixel pause criterion, at least in the simulator.

S3
Measured

Draw from the DOM, not from the engine's model. In Safari the engine's model is exact on justified lines, but it sets the last, ragged line up to 5 px off. It measures a space as the width of “A A” minus “AA”, and that includes the kerning between A and a space. In Chrome its widths differ slightly from the page's, so a justified line drifts by up to half a pixel by its end, and the last line by 2.5 px. Reading back from the DOM costs one pass per page, done once while the page rests.

S4
Measured

Selection still works. iOS's own handles and highlight sit on the transparent DOM text, over the drawn ink (below, with a light letterpress shading switched on).

S5
JudgementCheck

Still open. Speed on a real phone: in the simulator, drawing a full page of ink took about 1 ms and the upload and first draw 59 ms for a full-colour 1206 × 2142 texture. Built

One sheet: grip, edge and ink

The second pass on the leaf, after the feedback that you can't take the page at different points, it has no thickness, and highlights look like boxes, not ink. One model of the sheet now feeds the gesture, the renderer, the shade and the clip. All of it is behind Debug.

B1
Physics

A frame for the sheet. The simulated strip is the page's cross-section. A sheet frame (paper/sheet-frame.ts) lays it over the page along rulings that meet at an apex on the spine's line, beyond the page. With the apex far away the rulings are parallel, a roll. With it near, they fan, a cone, as in Hong, Card & Chen (C2). The spine is itself a ruling, so it stays flat, and the lift line is a ruling, so it stays straight (C5). Each strip segment is drawn as a flat facet, bent about its ruling, so the bent leaf is developable whatever the strip does (C4).

B2
Judgement

Held where it's taken. The finger keeps the paper point it touched (C19 for a fresh grip as well as a catch). The fold turns to lie across the pull, and it straightens as the leaf falls. A corner pulled up and across makes a diagonal fold. The middle of the edge pulled across rolls straight, because the apex can't come near without the corners trailing (C6). A grip well inside the page folds the outer part over it. A tap still pushes the fore-edge, and a backward turn is still a roll.

The apex is kept at least 0.3 of the page width off the page, and the fold turns by at most 42°. Past those, paper near the apex would have to bend tighter than a page does.

B3
PhysicsJudgement

Thickness. Book paper is about 0.1 mm thick, a third of a pixel at phone scale. The leaf draws its cut edges as a rim 0.9 px thick that reaches 0.6 px past the face, so the edge reads as a hairline where the leaf lifts. The book around the page shows the stacks: the unread leaves' edges as a band at the fore-edge that narrows as you read, and the read stack, off the screen beyond the spine, shading the gutter more as it grows. The turning leaf is the page less the band, so it lands on the stack's edge.

This is the honest limit of a phone: a page seen from straight above shows no thickness, and the left stack is off the screen. Only its shade reaches this page.

B4
PhysicsJudgement

Highlights as ink. A highlighter is a translucent dye laid by a felt tip, not a box of colour behind the words. Each highlighted line is now one stroke, drawn in WebGL and multiplied into the sheet under the text. Light passes through the dye (Beer–Lambert), so a pool deepens the hue as well as the darkness. Its edges are soft and uneven and soak a little way along the page's own grain. A marker's stroke has streaks along it, a pool where the tip touched, a chisel slant at its ends and a faint tide line. On dark paper the dye glows faintly instead. The marks keep their place in the page with a clear fill, so selection, hit-testing, the highlight tools and saved offsets don't change. The page raster carries the strokes onto the turning leaf and through to its back.
